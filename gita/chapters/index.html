Bhagavad Gita

Sanskrit, translation, and a plain-language explanation for each verse — kept clearly separate — cross-referenced against the concepts (karma, dharma, atman...) each verse teaches. Building chapter by chapter, starting with Chapter 2.

1

Arjuna Viṣāda Yoga — The Despondency of Arjuna

The armies of the Kurus and Pândavas stand arrayed at Kurukshetra. Surveying the battlefield, Arjuna sees his own teachers, elders, and kinsmen arrayed on both sides and is overwhelmed by grief, laying out at length his case against fighting — the crisis of conscience that Krishna's teaching in the following chapters directly responds to.

2

Sāṅkhya Yoga — The Yoga of Knowledge

Krishna's first direct teaching to the grief-stricken Arjuna: the immortality of the soul (atman), the duty argument for fighting (svadharma), action without attachment to its results (karma yoga), and the character of the sage of steady wisdom (sthitaprajna) — the philosophical foundation the later sixteen chapters build on.

72 / 72 verses
3

Karma Yoga — The Yoga of Action

Arjuna reopens the question left dangling at the end of chapter 2: if understanding is superior to action, why does Krishna keep steering him toward war? Krishna answers with the discourse's central teaching — no one can ever be truly actionless, since the gunas of nature compel everyone to act, so the real discipline is acting without attachment, as an offering (yajna), for the sake of the world's cohesion (lokasangraha) rather than personal gain. He cites King Janaka and his own conduct as proof that even the liberated act, precisely to set the standard others will follow. The chapter closes by naming desire and anger, born of rajas, as the wise person's true enemy — an appetite that clouds judgment "like a fire shrouded in smoke" and can only be mastered by disciplining the senses and standing firm in the self beyond even the intellect.

4

Jñāna Vibhāga Yoga — The Yoga of Wisdom

Chapter 4 opens with Krishna revealing the ancient, forgotten lineage of this teaching and the doctrine of avatara — his repeated descent whenever dharma decays — then explains how enlightened action leaves no karmic trace, since one who sees inaction in action acts through equanimity. The long middle stretch catalogues wildly different practices — deity-worship, sense-restraint, breath-control, wealth, austerity, study — as so many forms of yajna (sacrifice), all valid because all are, ultimately, karma directed toward the same end: the sacrifice of wisdom, which surpasses every other because all action culminates in jnana. The closing verses describe how wisdom is obtained (through a teacher, inquiry, and service), what it does (purifies completely, burns all past action to ash), and what its absence costs (the doubting self is destroyed) — ending on the command to cut ignorance-born doubt with "the sword of knowledge" and stand up, resolving the paralysis that opened the Gita in Chapter 1.

42 / 42 verses
5

Sannyāsa Yoga — The Yoga of the Renunciation of Action

Chapter 5 opens with Arjuna's question about whether renunciation of action or disciplined action (karma-yoga) is superior; Krishna answers that both lead to the same goal but yoga-by-action is more practical, since true renunciation is an inner disposition — acting without the sense of "I am the doer" — rather than physical withdrawal. The middle verses build a metaphysics of non-doership paired with the famous "equal-seeing" portrait of the sage who regards a learned brahmana, a cow, an elephant, a dog, and an outcaste with the same undivided vision, then turns inward to argue that contact-born pleasures are transient while a self-sufficient joy is found by withdrawing attention from the senses — previewing chapter 6's meditation technique. The chapter's final verse breaks from its impersonal register to identify Krishna directly as the friend (suhrit) of all beings, closing on peace (shanti).

29 / 29 verses
6

Adhyātma Yoga — The Yoga of Self-Subdual

Krishna gives Arjuna a detailed manual for meditation practice — a clean quiet seat, controlled posture, gaze fixed at the nose-tip, moderation in eating and sleep — building toward the mind's mastery over itself and the vision of the Self abiding equally in all beings. When Arjuna objects that the restless mind makes this goal unreachable, Krishna concedes the difficulty but insists it is attainable through practice (abhyasa) and dispassion (vairagya); when Arjuna then asks what becomes of one who strives sincerely but dies before perfecting the discipline, Krishna assures him that no effort toward the good is ever lost, carrying forward across rebirths until the goal is reached.

47 / 47 verses
7

Jñāna Yoga — The Yoga of Discriminative Knowledge

Krishna promises Arjuna knowledge so complete that nothing further remains to be known, then lays out the metaphysics that backs the promise: an eightfold lower nature (the material elements plus mind, intellect, and ego) and a higher nature that is the life-principle sustaining the universe, with himself as the source both spring from and dissolve into. A long immanence passage casts him as the defining essence within ordinary things — taste in water, radiance in sun and moon, sound in ether — before the chapter turns to how people relate to that reality: a fourfold typology of worshippers, the divine illusion (maya) that keeps most people from recognizing him, and the desire-driven detours through lesser gods that Krishna still honors but ranks below direct devotion. It closes on a six-term list — Brahman, adhyatma, karma, adhibhuta, adhidaiva, adhiyajna — that primes the opening question of chapter 8.

30 / 30 verses
8

Akṣara Brahma Yoga — The Yoga of the Indestructible Supreme Eternal

Arjuna opens by asking Krishna to define six technical terms — brahman, adhyatma, karma, adhibhuta, adhidaiva, adhiyajna — and what determines a person's destiny at death. Krishna answers each in turn, then centers the discourse on a single doctrine: whatever one remembers at the final moment of death is what one attains, making constant remembrance and yogic practice throughout life the real discipline. The chapter builds outward from meditation technique to cosmology — the vast day/night cycle of Brahma's world, a higher imperishable (akshara) beyond even that realm, and the two paths of light and darkness that determine return to rebirth or freedom from it — closing with a call to remain steady in yoga at all times.

28 / 28 verses
9

Rājavidyā Rājaguhya Yoga — The Yoga of the Kingly Science and the Kingly Secret

Chapter 9 opens with Krishna promising Arjuna the most secret knowledge — that he pervades and sustains the entire cosmos while remaining metaphysically untouched by it, unmanifest yet the womb of all beings. The middle verses develop this immanence/transcendence tension and sharpen into a critique of Vedic ritualists who transact with Krishna for heaven without recognizing him. The closing arc resolves that critique: ritual heaven is temporary and cyclical, but Krishna accepts even the simplest offering — a leaf, a flower, fruit, water — given in devotion, and this devotional path is declared open even to those the varna hierarchy marginalized (women, vaishyas, shudras), a genuinely progressive claim for its era though not a rejection of the hierarchy itself. The chapter ends with a direct summons to fix the mind on Krishna alone, a formula the text echoes verbatim near its close in chapter 18.

34 / 34 verses
10

Vibhūti Yoga — The Yoga of Sovereignty

Arjuna asks Krishna to describe his own divine glories in fuller detail, and Krishna responds with an extended catalogue of manifestations — declaring himself the foremost member of category after category, from the Ganges among rivers to the Himalaya among mountains to the syllable Om among speech — as a way of teaching Arjuna to recognize him in the world. The chapter closes by insisting the list is only illustrative, not exhaustive, and that any glorious or beautiful thing at all springs from a mere fragment of Krishna's splendor, since one fragment of him already sustains the entire universe. That closing image of partial, fragmentary pervasion is precisely what leaves Arjuna unsatisfied and drives his request in Chapter 11 to see Krishna's whole form directly.

42 / 42 verses
11

Viśvarūpa Darśana Yoga — The Yoga of the Vision of the Universal Form

Arjuna, having asked to see Krishna's true form, is shown the terrifying vishwarupa — an infinite body containing all worlds, gods, and time itself, its many mouths devouring the very warriors who are about to die in the war ahead. At the vision's climax Krishna declares "kalo'smi lokakshayakrit" — I am Time, the destroyer of worlds — reframing the impending battle as an outcome already decided, with Arjuna cast as instrument rather than cause. Arjuna's terror gives way to an extended hymn of praise, then to genuine embarrassment at having once addressed this infinite being as a casual friend, and finally to a plea for the vision to end. Krishna obliges in stages — first the four-armed form, then the ordinary human one — and closes the chapter by teaching that this cosmic form can be known only through undivided devotion (bhakti), not through Vedic study, sacrifice, austerity, or charity.

55 / 55 verses
12

Bhakti Yoga — The Yoga of Devotion

Arjuna asks whether devotees who worship Krishna's personal form or those who meditate on the unmanifest, formless absolute are the better yogis. Krishna answers that both reach him, but devotion to the personal form is easier for embodied beings, since fixing the mind on the unmanifest is exceptionally hard — and sketches a graded path (practice, then working for his sake, then simply renouncing the fruit of action) for anyone who can't yet manage direct concentration. The chapter closes with a long portrait of the qualities that mark a devotee dear to him: free of ill-will, friendly, compassionate, equanimous in pleasure and pain, forgiving, content, and the same toward friend and foe, praise and blame.

13

Kṣetra Kṣetrajña Vibhāga Yoga — The Yoga of the Distinction Between the Field and the Knower of the Field

Arjuna asks Krishna to explain prakriti and purusha, and the Field (kshetra) and its Knower (kshetrajna). Krishna defines the body as the Field and the perceiving self as its Knower, catalogs the Field's material and psychological components alongside the qualities that constitute true wisdom, then describes the object of that wisdom — the beginningless, paradoxical Brahman that is neither being nor non-being — before establishing prakriti and purusha as two co-eternal principles: nature that performs all action and spirit that merely witnesses and experiences it. The chapter closes by showing that seeing this distinction, and seeing the Self as an actionless witness untouched by nature's activity, is what constitutes liberation, setting up chapter 14's detailed treatment of the three gunas.

35 / 35 verses
14

Guṇatraya Vibhāga Yoga — The Yoga of Separation from the Three Qualities

Krishna explains the three gunas born of prakriti — sattva, rajas, and tamas — that bind the indestructible dweller in the body, describing how each guna manifests, which fruits and destinies it produces, and which guna dominant at death determines the character of the next birth. The chapter turns on verse 19's insight that liberation lies in the Seer recognizing the gunas' activity as never its own, then answers Arjuna's question with a portrait of the gunatita — one who has crossed beyond the three qualities entirely — before naming exclusive devotion (bhakti yoga) as the actual means, and closing with Krishna identifying himself as the very ground of Brahman, immortality, eternal dharma, and bliss.

27 / 27 verses
15

Puruṣottama Yoga — The Yoga of Attaining the Supreme Spirit

Krishna opens with the striking image of an inverted cosmic ashvattha tree (roots above, branches below) as a figure for the world to be cut down with the axe of non-attachment, then explains the true Purusha behind the world: an eternal fragment of himself becomes the individual embodied soul, drawing in mind and senses the way wind carries scent, and it is Krishna's own light that lights the sun, moon, and fire. The chapter closes with its title-verse: beyond the perishable (all beings) and the imperishable (the changeless witness) stands a highest Purusha, Purushottama, who pervades and sustains both — and whoever knows this, undeluded, "hath finished his work" and knows all there is to know.

20 / 20 verses
16

Daivāsura Sampad Vibhāga Yoga — The Yoga of the Division Between the Divine and the Demoniacal

Chapter 16 opens mid-discourse with Krishna cataloguing roughly two dozen "divine" qualities across three verses, then compressing the "demonic" nature into a single terse verse of six traits — an asymmetry that runs through the chapter. Verses 6-20 stay almost entirely with the demonic type: nihilistic, godless cosmology, insatiable desire and unjust ambition, self-deifying egoism, and the consequence — repeated rebirth into demonic wombs and a fall into hell. Verse 21 delivers the practical hinge — lust, anger, and greed as the "triple gate of hell" to be renounced — and the chapter closes by making scripture the explicit authority for right action, a note Chapter 17 immediately complicates.

24 / 24 verses
17

Śraddhātraya Vibhāga Yoga — The Yoga of the Division of Threefold Faith

Arjuna opens by asking about the status of those who sacrifice with faith but outside scriptural ordinance, and Krishna answers by sidestepping the binary entirely: faith itself is threefold by nature (sattvic, rajasic, tamasic), and this same guna-typology is then applied systematically across objects of worship, food, sacrifice, austerity, and gift-giving — directly paralleling the guna framework of Chapter 14 and previewing the closing typology of Chapter 18. The chapter closes with an exposition of the "Om Tat Sat" three-syllable formula as the threefold name of Brahman used to consecrate ritual acts, ending on the declaration that anything done without faith (shraddha) is "Asat" — null and void, here or hereafter.

28 / 28 verses
18

Mokṣa Sannyāsa Yoga — The Yoga of Liberation by Renunciation

The Gita's final chapter opens with Arjuna asking Krishna to finally distinguish sannyasa (renunciation) from tyaga (relinquishment), and Krishna answers by systematically re-running the guna-typology of chapters 14 and 17 across renunciation, action, knowledge, the actor, intellect, resolve, happiness, and finally the four varnas' duties — arguing that perfection comes through wholehearted engagement in one's own duty (svadharma) regardless of its content, echoing 3.35 almost verbatim. It then traces the path from purified reason through meditation to Brahman-attainment, which resolves into supreme bhakti rather than static abstraction, building to Krishna's climactic personal appeal and the Gita's single most consequential command: total surrender in exchange for liberation from all sin. The chapter — and the entire text — closes by resolving its own frame narrative: Arjuna's doubt-free declaration to act, directly reversing his chapter-1 collapse, and Sanjaya's closing testimony that wherever Krishna and Arjuna stand together, victory is assured.
