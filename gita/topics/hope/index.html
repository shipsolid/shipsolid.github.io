Hope

Core Human Emotions & Spiritual Insights

This one needs an honest disclaimer before anything else: the Gita doesn’t really have a concept of hope in the everyday English sense — optimism about a specific desired outcome. Where the word closest to it appears (6.10), it’s listed among things to be free of, alongside greed. What follows is not a direct translation of “hope” but an interpretive angle — the Gita’s substitute for it, built out of confidence that sincere effort is never wasted rather than optimism about any particular result.

What the Gita asks you to let go of

BG 6.10 instructs the yogi to practice “free from hope and greed” (nirāśīr, aparigrahaḥ) as part of the setup for meditation. Read carefully, this is aimed at anxious expectation and craving for a specific result, not at forward-looking confidence in general — it sits in the same breath as freedom from possessiveness, both aimed at loosening grip, not at pessimism.

The reassurance underneath

BG 6.40 answers Arjuna’s fear of failure directly: “neither in this world nor in the life to come is there destruction for him… never doth any who worketh righteousness tread the path of woe.” This is the Gita’s real substitute for hope — not confidence that a specific outcome will arrive, but an unconditional guarantee that sincere striving toward the good is never simply lost.

Across as many lifetimes as it takes

BG 6.45 extends that guarantee across “manifold births” before the “supreme goal” is reached. The timescale matters: this isn’t a promise calibrated to near-term optimism, it’s a much slower, more durable confidence than ordinary hope usually carries.

Relevant Shlokas

6.10

Let the Yogi constantly engage himself in yoga, remaining in a secret place by himself, with thought and self subdued, free from hope and greed.

meditationsadhana
6.40

O son of Prithâ, neither in this world nor in the life to come is there destruction for him; never doth any who worketh righteousness, O beloved, tread the path of woe.

reassurancekarma
6.45

But the Yogi, labouring with assiduity, purified from sin, fully perfected through manifold births, he reacheth the supreme goal.

rebirthreassurance

Practical Lessons

  • Be precise about what's being renounced at 6.10: nirāśīr describes freedom from anxious expectation and craving for a result, paired directly with freedom from greed — not a general ban on all forward-looking confidence.
  • 6.40's reassurance is unconditional, not hedged: 'neither in this world nor in the life to come' is destruction possible for someone who has worked righteously. If you're anxious about wasted effort, this is the Gita's direct answer, not a vague comfort.
  • 6.45 explicitly allows for the goal to take 'manifold births' to reach — the Gita's substitute for hope is built for a far longer timeline than most everyday hope is calibrated for. Don't import next-quarter expectations onto a promise scaled in lifetimes.
  • If you want a durable version of hope from this text, anchor it to effort's non-wastage (6.40, 6.45), not to a specific near-term outcome — the Gita is far more willing to guarantee the first than the second.
