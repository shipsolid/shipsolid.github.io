Motivation

Self-Growth & Strength

The Gita’s teaching on motivation runs through one of its most quoted lines, but the surrounding verses matter just as much — they diagnose exactly what kind of motivation is being replaced before the replacement is offered.

What’s being diagnosed

BG 2.43 names the pattern directly: desire for self, heaven as the goal, elaborate ceremony as the means to pleasure and status. BG 4.12 names the same pattern from a different angle — people seek quick results through transactional worship precisely because, in the human world, results from action really do arrive fast. Neither verse claims this kind of motivation fails to work. The claim is narrower and more interesting: it works, and it’s still the lower-order version.

Where motivation is allowed to come from

BG 2.47 is the answer, and it’s worth reading precisely rather than as a general “don’t care about results” slogan. “Thy business is with the action only, never with its fruits” relocates where motivation is allowed to originate — from the doing itself, not from what the doing will earn you. The verse immediately closes the two obvious misreadings: don’t make the fruit your motive, and don’t use indifference to fruit as an excuse to stop acting. It asks for full engagement with the action and a genuinely lowered stake in its result — not less effort, differently sourced effort.

Related

  • Karma and Yoga — 2.47’s formula is the founding statement of karma yoga, the disciplined, fruit-detached mode of action this topic’s central verse names outright.

Relevant Shlokas

2.43

With desire for self, with heaven for goal, they offer birth as the fruit of action, and prescribe many and various ceremonies for the attainment of pleasure and lordship.

karmaritualism
4.12

They who long after success in action on earth worship the Shining Ones; for in brief space verily, in this world of men, success is born of action.

ritualism
2.47

Thy business is with the action only, never with its fruits; so let not the fruit of action be thy motive, nor be thou to inaction attached.

karma-yoganon-attachment

Practical Lessons

  • 2.43 diagnoses a specific motivational pattern before the Gita prescribes anything: acting for a good rebirth, pleasure, or status through the 'right' rituals. Notice that the criticism isn't of wanting good outcomes — it's of building your entire motivation to act around securing them.
  • 4.12 makes the same diagnosis in blunter terms: people turn to quick, transactional motivation because results-in-this-world really do come fast. The Gita doesn't deny this works — it names it as a lower-order motivation precisely because it works, not despite it.
  • 2.47's actual claim is narrower than 'don't care about outcomes' — it's 'don't let the outcome be your motive.' You're explicitly told not to become attached to inaction either. The instruction is to relocate the source of your effort, not to lower your effort.
  • A practical test: if the outcome disappeared entirely — no credit, no result, no proof it mattered — would you still be motivated to do the action itself? 2.47's version of motivation is the kind that survives that question; 2.43's isn't.
