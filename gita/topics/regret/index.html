Regret

Challenges & Inner Struggles

Regret turns out to be genuinely thin territory in the Gita, and it’s worth saying that plainly rather than forcing a clean answer where one doesn’t exist. The text has a precise vocabulary for doubt (saṃśaya), for grief (śoka), and for delusion (moha) — but no equivalent dedicated term for the specific, retrospective feeling of wishing a past choice undone. What follows connects the closest real moments the text offers, honestly labeled as an interpretive reading rather than a verse-by-verse teaching on regret as such.

Anticipating the regret before it arrives

BG 1.31 has Arjuna reasoning forward rather than back: “nor do I foresee any advantage from slaying kinsmen in battle.” This is regret’s earlier, more useful cousin — running the likely aftermath before acting, not after. Most of what people call “avoiding regret” in practice is really this: doing the forward-looking version of the reckoning while there’s still a choice to make.

The moment closest to regret in real time

BG 11.45 is the text’s nearest approach to regret as it’s usually meant — a wish to reverse a choice just made. Having asked Krishna to reveal the full cosmic form, Arjuna is immediately undone by it and asks for the ordinary form back. There’s no self-condemnation attached to the request; it’s presented as a plain, reasonable reaction to a consequence landing harder than expected, not a moral failing to be ashamed of.

Krishna’s actual prescription: deliberate first

BG 18.63 closes one of the text’s major teaching movements with a handoff: “having reflected on it fully, then act as thou listest.” Read against how much of chapters 1 and 2 is spent on exactly the kind of anguish that follows a rushed, ungrounded choice, this reads less like permissiveness and more like the Gita’s real answer to regret — not a way to feel better about a decision after it’s made, but an argument for putting the reflection where it can still change the outcome.

Practical Lessons

  • This is thin territory and worth naming as such: the Gita doesn't have a dedicated term for regret the way it has saṃśaya for doubt. What it has instead are moments adjacent to regret — anticipating a bad outcome before acting, and wanting to undo a choice just made — which is a different, earlier-stage thing than classic after-the-fact remorse.
  • 1.31 is Arjuna reasoning forward, not backward: 'I do not foresee any good from slaying kinsmen,' stated before the battle, not after. Treat this as the more useful half of regret to actually practice — running the tape forward before committing, rather than only running it backward afterward.
  • 11.45 is the closest the text comes to real-time regret: having asked to see Krishna's terrifying cosmic form, Arjuna immediately asks for it to stop and for the familiar form back. The lesson isn't 'don't ask for things' — it's that asking to reverse a choice the moment its consequences land is a normal, undramatic response, not a special failure.
  • 18.63 is Krishna's own closing move on this territory, and it's a prevention strategy rather than a cure: 'having reflected on it fully, then act as you choose.' The Gita's actual answer to regret isn't a technique for feeling better afterward — it's spending the deliberation upfront, before the choice is made, precisely because undoing it afterward usually isn't on offer.
