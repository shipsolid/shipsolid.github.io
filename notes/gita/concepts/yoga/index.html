Yoga (योग)

In the Gita's second chapter, yoga names a discipline of evenness of mind in action — not a physical practice, and not a single technique — first given this technical sense at BG 2.48.

In contemporary usage “yoga” mostly means a physical practice. In the Bhagavad Gita’s second chapter it means something closer to discipline or union — and the specific discipline in question, karma yoga, is named directly at BG 2.48: “yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam” — yoga is skill in action, defined a few words earlier as evenness of mind (samatvam) toward success and failure alike.

Not a technique, a stance

This is easy to miss if “yoga” arrives already loaded with modern connotations. The Gita’s chapter 2 sense is not a set of postures or breathing exercises — it’s a stance one brings to whatever action is already in front of you: perform it fully, established in equanimity, without letting the anticipated result become your motive for acting (see Karma (कर्म) and BG 2.47). Later chapters of the Gita go on to describe other paths — bhakti yoga, jnana yoga — but chapter 2 is where the term is first given technical content.

Related notes

Karma (कर्म)

The Gita's technical sense of karma — action itself, and the discipline of acting without attachment to its fruit — distinct from the popular "cosmic reward and punishment" meaning of the word.

Dharma (धर्म)

One's own duty (svadharma) in the Gita's second chapter — a role-bound, contextual obligation, not a single universal rule for everyone.

Atman (आत्मन्)

The self that inhabits the body in the Gita's second chapter — unborn, imperishable, and untouched by what happens to the body — the metaphysical foundation the chapter's argument for fighting rests on.

Sthitaprajna (स्थितप्रज्ञ)

The "person of steady wisdom" the Gita's second chapter closes with — its technical term for what settled equanimity actually looks like, in mind and action.
